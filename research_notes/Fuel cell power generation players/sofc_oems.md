# SOFC OEMs for Stationary / Utility-Scale Power Generation

## Product lines, power range, module configuration, temperature, fuel flexibility (incl. pure H2 vs. NG-only)

### Takeaway
Bloom Energy (US) is the largest commercial SOFC OEM by deployed MW and is the only vendor with a widely documented, commercially offered pure-hydrogen product (natural-gas, biogas and blended/pure-H₂ variants of the same platform). Mitsubishi Power/MHI (Japan) is unique in offering a pressurized SOFC integrated with a micro gas turbine (SOFC-MGT hybrid) rather than a standalone SOFC, and is separately one of the world's largest heavy-duty gas turbine OEMs. Ceres Power (UK) does not sell hardware directly — it licenses its planar SOFC/SOEC stack technology to manufacturing partners (Bosch, Doosan Fuel Cell, Weichai, Delta Electronics/Cummins previously). Convion (Finland, using Elcogen stacks) and SolydEra (Italy, ex-SolidPower) serve the sub-MW commercial/industrial CHP segment and explicitly market hydrogen-fuel-capable variants of natural-gas/biogas systems. Sunfire (Germany) has moved its commercial focus to reversible SOFC/SOEC (power-to-gas and gas-to-power) rather than pure power generation. Toshiba Energy Systems (Japan) is primarily a hydrogen-supply-chain/PEM integrator (H2One, H2Rex) with SOFC/SOEC still at an earlier R&D stage, not a commercial stationary-SOFC power OEM in the Bloom/Mitsubishi sense.

### Cited Findings
- Bloom Energy's Energy Server platform (current commercial generation, "Server 6.5" in the most recent public datasheet found) is fuel-flexible: natural gas, biogas, blended hydrogen, or pure hydrogen — [Bloom Energy Server Datasheet 2024](https://www.bloomenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/bloom-energy-server-datasheet-2024.pdf); [Bloom Energy Technology page](https://www.bloomenergy.com/technology/)
- Base commercial module/block size for Bloom's global product is 325 kW (net AC nameplate); systems are built up in 325 kW blocks to reach MW-plus plant scale — [Bloom Energy Server Datasheet 2024](https://www.bloomenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/bloom-energy-server-datasheet-2024.pdf)
- Bloom announced a dedicated Hydrogen-Powered Energy Server product; hydrogen spec sheet lists output "300 kW and above," 3-phase 480 V AC, fuel = pure hydrogen (≥99.90% purity) — [Bloom Energy Hydrogen Data Sheet](https://www.bloomenergy.com/resource/hydrogen-spec-sheet/); [Bloom Energy Announces Hydrogen-Powered Energy Servers](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-announces-hydrogen-powered-energy-servers-to-make-always-on-renewable-electricity-a-reality/)
- Bloom's platform is described as a non-combustion electrochemical conversion (no combustion step), operating on the SOFC principle — [Bloom Energy Technology page](https://www.bloomenergy.com/technology/)
- Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI)/Mitsubishi Power's product is a **pressurized SOFC-micro gas turbine (MGT) hybrid**, not a standalone fuel cell: MHI achieved 4,000 hours continuous operation of a 200 kW-class pressurized SOFC-MGT hybrid, generating-end output 206 kW AC — [MHI: World's First 4,000-Hour Continuous Operation of Pressurized SOFC-MGT Hybrid](https://www.mhi.com/news/1309201714.html)
- MHPS (Mitsubishi Hitachi Power Systems, predecessor brand) launched an "Integrated Fuel Cell and Gas Turbine Hybrid Power Generation System" product line in 2017 and received its first commercial order in Jan 2018, installed at the Marunouchi Building, Tokyo (owned/operated by Mitsubishi Estate) — [MHPS Launches New Integrated Fuel Cell and Gas Turbine Hybrid Power Generation System](https://power.mhi.com/news/20170809.html); [MHPS Receives First Order for Integrated Fuel Cell and Gas Turbine Hybrid Power Generation System](https://power.mhi.com/news/20180131.html)
- Mitsubishi Power's SOFC-hybrid concept scales from "medium-capacity (250 kW class) distributed power sources to large-capacity centralized power sources," envisioning integration with utility-scale GTCC (gas turbine combined cycle) and IGCC plants — [MHI Technical Review, "Efforts toward Introduction of SOFC-MGT Hybrid System"](https://www.mhi.com/technology/review/sites/g/files/jwhtju2326/files/tr/pdf/e554/e554170.pdf)
- Mitsubishi Power is **separately** a major heavy-duty industrial/utility gas turbine OEM (the same corporate group but a distinct, much larger business line from its SOFC-hybrid product); note this distinction per the assignment brief (see also MHI's SOEC hydrogen-production test module at Takasago Hydrogen Park, begun April 2024, 400 kW — related but distinct electrolysis technology) — [MHI Begins Operation of SOEC Test Module at Takasago Hydrogen Park](https://power.mhi.com/news/240425.html)
- Ceres Power operates an **asset-light licensing model**: it does not manufacture/sell finished SOFC power plants; it licenses its planar SOFC (and SOEC) stack IP and cell technology to industrial partners who manufacture and commercialize the hardware — [Ceres Power — Bloom Energy of Asia and Europe in the Making](https://ponderanceofevidence.substack.com/p/ceres-power-bloom-energy-of-asia); [Ceres Power Analysis 2025: Inside the SOFC/SOEC Pivot](https://enkiai.com/fuel-cells/ceres-power-analysis-2025-inside-the-sofc-soec-pivot/)
- Doosan Fuel Cell (South Korea), a Ceres licensee, began **mass production** of fuel cell stacks using Ceres technology at a dedicated South Korean factory in July 2025, with annual capacity of 50 MW — [Doosan Fuel Cell and Ceres joint announcement, 28 July 2025](https://www.ceres.tech/media/1ppfrodd/external_ceres-power-doosan-sop-announcement-20250728-final.pdf)
- Weichai (China), a large industrial conglomerate (revenue well above $20bn), signed a manufacturing licence with Ceres in November 2025 to produce SOFC cells/stacks in China for stationary power markets including AI data centres, commercial buildings and industrial applications — [Weichai sign manufacturing licence for SOFC power — Ceres Power](https://www.ceres.tech/newsroom/news-and-insights/weichai-sign-manufacturing-licence-for-sofc-power/); [Weichai Signs Manufacturing Licence RNS, 5 Nov 2025](https://www.ceres.tech/media/2dtcn4ns/weichai-signs-manufacturing-licence-for-sofc-power-rns.pdf)
- Ceres's planned China joint venture with **Bosch** did not conclude in 2024; the Weichai direct licence followed in 2025 — [Ceres Power 2025: SOFC Mass Market Breakthrough](https://enkiai.com/fuel-cells/ceres-power-2025-sofc-mass-market-breakthrough/)
- Convion's C50 SOFC system is a ~58 kW-rated power module for CHP on natural gas or biogas, with stable net electric output between 25–55 kW and electric efficiency 50–55% across that range — [Electric and total efficiencies of the first Convion C50 SOFC system, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Electric-and-total-efficiencies-of-the-first-Convion-C50-SOFC-system-as-function-of-the_fig2_341720840)
- Convion's C60 system produces 60 kW electricity plus ~25 kW recoverable heat, with electricity-production efficiency of ~60%; it can be configured to run on natural gas, biogas, **or hydrogen** — [Elcogen's partner Convion brings its C60 fuel cell system to market](https://elcogen.com/elcogens-partner-convion-brings-its-c60-fuel-cell-system-to-market/)
- Convion uses Elcogen (Estonia/Finland) as its stack supplier — [Convion Products page](https://convion.fi/products/)
- Convion has also operated a first SOEC (electrolysis, reverse-mode) system in Espoo, Finland, producing hydrogen at VTT's Bioruukki test site (adjacent capability, not the C50/C60 power product) — [Convion C50 product status update](https://convion.fi/convion-c50-product-status-update/)
- SolydEra (Italy, formerly SolidPower's stationary business) markets the elcoStack-class G8X-10 planar stack platform, rated 10 kW in SOFC (power) mode and 25 kW in SOE (electrolysis) mode, described as "the largest stack commercially available" at time of the Hannover Messe 2025 listing; SOFC mode runs on multiple fuels and generates electricity+heat, while SOE mode produces hydrogen from renewable electricity (reversible) — [HANNOVER MESSE 2025 Product: STACK G8X-10 (SolydEra)](https://www.hannovermesse.de/product/stack-g8x-10/460858/N1569079)
- SolydEra also lists a smaller 1.5 kW SOFC (4.5 kW SOE) stack and an 8 kW SOFC (25 kW SOE) stack in its portfolio, targeted at micro-grid/residential CHP scale rather than utility scale — [SolydEra Business Areas and Solutions](https://www.solydera.com/en/business-areas-solutions/)
- Sunfire (Germany) has shifted its commercial stack/module product line toward **reversible SOFC/SOEC** systems that switch between power generation and hydrogen (power-to-gas) modes, targeting European green-hydrogen hubs, rather than a dedicated pure power-generation SOFC line — [Frontiers: Progress and outlook of SOFC technology for stationary power generation](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2025.1650696/full)
- Elcogen (Estonia/Finland/UK) is a **stack-level** manufacturer (supplies cells/stacks to systems integrators such as Convion) rather than a systems OEM in its own right; its elcoStack E3000 G2 operates 650–750°C, functions in both electrolysis and fuel-cell mode, and runs on hydrogen, biofuels, or natural gas — [Elcogen elcoStack E3000 G2, gasworld](https://www.gasworld.com/story/elcogen-launches-solid-oxide-stack-for-hydrogen-production-and-fuel-flexible-power/2249335.article/)
- Toshiba Energy Systems' flagship commercially deployed hydrogen products are **H2One** (integrated hydrogen generation/storage/fuel-cell/battery system, PEM-fuel-cell based) and **H2Rex** (a hydrogen fuel-cell power-generation system); Toshiba's solid-oxide work (SOEC hydrogen production at 700 W, 3.5 kW and 100 kW scales) appears to be R&D-stage rather than a commercialized stationary-SOFC power product — [Toshiba: Use Hydrogen — Products and technical services](https://www.global.toshiba/ww/products-solutions/hydrogen/products-technical-services/fuel-cell.html); [NEDO: Development of Solid Oxide Electrolysis Cell System](https://www.nedo.go.jp/content/100939729.pdf)
- Toshiba's H2One system was installed and began full-scale operation at Toranomon Hills Business Tower, Tokyo, in 2020, and Toshiba's H2One was separately selected to support hydrogen/energy-sustainability research efforts in Singapore (2019 announcement) — [Toshiba integrated hydrogen energy system starts operation at Toranomon Hills, Green Car Congress](https://www.greencarcongress.com/2020/06/20200621-toshiba.html); [Toshiba's H2One Selected to Support Singapore's Research Efforts in Energy Sustainability](https://www.global.toshiba/ww/news/energy/2019/10/news-20191030-01.html)

### Inferences
- Only Bloom Energy currently offers a clearly commercialized, MW-scale pure-hydrogen SOFC product line at published spec-sheet level; other vendors (Convion, SolydEra, Elcogen) describe hydrogen fuel-flexibility mainly at the sub-100 kW pilot/demo scale, and Mitsubishi Power's hybrid is documented on natural gas/hydrocarbon fuel, not hydrogen, in the sources found.
- The industry is bifurcating between (a) standalone SOFC power-generation OEMs (Bloom, Convion, SolydEra) and (b) reversible SOFC/SOEC players pivoting toward hydrogen production as much as (or more than) power generation (Sunfire, Ceres/partners' SOEC lines, SolydEra's SOE mode) — relevant to this project's ammonia-cracker-plus-H2-export chain only as an analogue technology, not as a direct component.
- Toshiba is best characterized in this survey as a hydrogen-systems integrator (PEM-based H2One/H2Rex) with SOFC/SOEC still emerging, not a peer competitor to Bloom/Mitsubishi Power in commercial stationary SOFC power generation.

### Gaps
- No specific product model name or datasheet was found for a Toshiba stationary SOFC power-generation product analogous to Bloom's Energy Server; only R&D-stage SOEC modules (700 W/3.5 kW/100 kW) were found — treat Toshiba as unconfirmed/likely not a commercially active SOFC power OEM.
- Precise current (2025/2026) model name and full spec sheet for Bloom's latest generation ("Server 7" was searched but not confirmed to exist in the sources found; the most recent public datasheet found references "Energy Server 6.5") — flag this discrepancy for the report writer rather than assuming a newer model exists.
- No data found on FuelCell Energy, Aisin, Kyocera, Cummins/Hydrogenics or other adjacent players' utility-scale SOFC offerings; these were not covered by this scoped search and may need a follow-up pass if in scope.

## Electrical efficiency (LHV/HHV basis), CHP/total efficiency, and SOFC-GT hybrid efficiency claims

### Takeaway
Bloom Energy publishes an LHV-basis average lifetime electrical efficiency of ~54% (with beginning-of-life up to ~65% and a stated efficiency guarantee of 52% cumulative), rising to >90% total efficiency with heat capture; a 60% net-AC electrical efficiency claim exists specifically for the 100%-hydrogen configuration. Mitsubishi Power's demonstrated SOFC-MGT hybrid achieved ~50.2% thermal efficiency at 200 kW-class scale in a real continuous-operation test, with CHP efficiency up to 65–73% when heat is recovered, and MHI's own literature projects 55–70% for future GTCC/IGCC-integrated large-scale hybrid concepts (these are projections, not measured results, and should be flagged as such). Convion's smaller C50/C60 systems report 50–60% electrical efficiency. The efficiency basis (LHV vs HHV) is not always stated explicitly in vendor marketing material — this is a recurring sourcing gap.

### Cited Findings
- Bloom Energy Server: cumulative electrical efficiency stated as 65–53% (LHV, net AC), described as declining from beginning-of-life over the system's life; total efficiency >90% when heat capture is included; "industry-leading average lifetime electrical efficiency of 54%" — [Bloom Energy Server Datasheet 2024](https://www.bloomenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/bloom-energy-server-datasheet-2024.pdf)
- Bloom's contractual Efficiency Guaranty in customer agreements is typically 52% measured cumulatively over the service term (some projects use tighter 45% monthly guarantees) — [Bloom Energy Corp 10-K/8-K SEC filings, cited via search summary](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1664703/000119312521051189/d24355dex991.htm)
- On 100% hydrogen fuel, Bloom Energy claims the SOFC platform "can now generate electricity with an incredible 60% efficiency" — [Interesting Engineering: US' Bloom Energy achieves 60% efficiency with hydrogen fuel cell](https://interestingengineering.com/energy/bloom-energy-fuel-cell-delivers-60-percent-efficiency)
- Bloom's pure-hydrogen data sheet specifies power generation efficiency "averaging 52% (initial maximum 60%)" for the pure-H₂ (≥99.90%) configuration, 300 kW and above — [Bloom Energy Hydrogen Data Sheet](https://www.bloomenergy.com/resource/hydrogen-spec-sheet/)
- Mitsubishi/MHI's 200 kW-class pressurized SOFC-MGT hybrid demonstrated 4,000 hours of continuous operation with generating-end output 206 kW AC and **50.2% thermal (electrical) efficiency** — a measured, not projected, figure — [MHI: World's First 4,000-Hour Continuous Operation of Pressurized SOFC-MGT Hybrid](https://www.mhi.com/news/1309201714.html)
- In cogeneration mode, MHI states combined (CHP) efficiency for the SOFC-MGT hybrid can reach as much as 65% or 73% when exhaust heat is recovered as steam or hot water — [MHI: 4,000-Hour Continuous Operation press release](https://www.mhi.com/news/1309201714.html)
- MHI's technical literature projects (not yet demonstrated at that scale) generation efficiencies of 60–70% for SOFC integrated with gas-turbine-combined-cycle (GTCC) on LNG, and 55–60% for SOFC integrated with IGCC on coal, at much larger centralized-power scale — [MHI Technical Review: Efforts toward Introduction of SOFC-MGT Hybrid System](https://www.mhi.com/technology/review/sites/g/files/jwhtju2326/files/tr/pdf/e554/e554170.pdf) — **flag as forward-looking/projected, not a currently operating commercial figure.**
- Convion C50: electric efficiency 50–55% across its stable output range (25–55 kW net) — [ResearchGate: Electric and total efficiencies of the first Convion C50 SOFC system](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Electric-and-total-efficiencies-of-the-first-Convion-C50-SOFC-system-as-function-of-the_fig2_341720840)
- Convion C60: electricity production efficiency ~60%, plus ~25 kW recoverable heat alongside 60 kW electric output — [Elcogen: Convion brings C60 to market](https://elcogen.com/elcogens-partner-convion-brings-its-c60-fuel-cell-system-to-market/)
- Elcogen's elcoStack E3000 G2 (stack-level, feeding into systems like Convion's) is stated to deliver up to 75% electrical efficiency in fuel-cell mode, or up to 90% with waste-heat utilization — [gasworld: Elcogen launches solid oxide stack](https://www.gasworld.com/story/elcogen-launches-solid-oxide-stack-for-hydrogen-production-and-fuel-flexible-power/2249335.article/) — note this is a stack-level (not fully-dressed balance-of-plant system) efficiency figure and may not be directly comparable to Bloom's or Convion's net-AC system-level numbers.

### Inferences
- Efficiency claims across vendors are not consistently reported on the same basis (net AC system-level vs. stack-level; LHV vs. unspecified) and are not always dated to a specific test or fleet snapshot — any comparison table built from these figures should footnote basis and measurement context per source.
- Mitsubishi Power's demonstrated (not projected) hybrid efficiency (~50.2%) is lower than Bloom's stated lifetime average (~54% LHV) and well below Bloom's peak/hydrogen claim (60%), but MHI's hybrid additionally captures far more usable heat (up to 73% CHP) than Bloom's baseline (>90% CHP is only with the optional heat-capture product).

### Gaps
- No explicit statement found of whether Mitsubishi Power's 50.2% and CHP 65–73% figures are LHV or HHV basis — flagged as a sourcing gap; report writer should present as "basis not stated in primary source."
- No large-scale (multi-MW), currently operating SOFC-GTCC hybrid efficiency was found as a measured (rather than projected) figure — the 55–65% GTCC-scale claims in MHI literature are explicitly projections for future integration, not results from an existing installed plant.
- Ceres Power, SolydEra and Sunfire electrical-efficiency figures at the finished-system (not stack) level were not found in this search pass; SolydEra/Elcogen figures found are stack-level only.

## Footprint (m²/MW or ft²/MW), CAPEX ($/kW), stack degradation/replacement interval

### Takeaway
Only Bloom Energy has any publicly discoverable footprint figure, and even that is a single third-party-summarized estimate (~1,151 ft²/MW ≈ 107 m² per MW) rather than a primary vendor spec sheet number confirmed in this pass — treat with caution and re-verify from the primary datasheet before using in a design brief. CAPEX figures are stale (2018-era ~$7,000–8,000/kW for a 100 kW Bloom unit from SEC S-1 filings) and no current $/kW figure was found; a 2026 pricing reference cites an operating cost of $0.099/kWh rather than installed capital cost. Stack replacement intervals have improved from Bloom's early ~12–18 months to a stated target of "over five years" from 2017 onward, though at least one contested third-party report claims real-world intervals closer to two years — this is a disputed figure and should be presented as such. No footprint or CAPEX data were found for Mitsubishi Power, Ceres/licensees, Convion, SolydEra, Sunfire, or Toshiba in this search pass.

### Cited Findings
- A third-party financial-analysis summary states Bloom Energy's footprint is "107 square meters" per MW, i.e., approximately 1,151 ft²/MW — [search-summarized from Bloom Energy Corp SEC S-1/A filings and related financial analysis, FY2018](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001664703/000119312518214115/d96446ds1a.htm) — **not independently confirmed against a primary Bloom spec sheet in this pass; the primary Bloom datasheet PDF could not be fetched directly (blocked) to verify.**
- Historical Bloom Energy Server capital cost: a 100 kW unit reportedly cost $700,000–$800,000, equating to roughly $7,000–$8,000/kW — sourced from search-result synthesis of Bloom Energy Corp SEC filings (S-1/A, FY2018) — [SEC EDGAR: Bloom Energy Corp S-1/A](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001664703/000119312518214115/d96446ds1a.htm)
- A more recent pricing reference states Bloom's Energy Server "starting from $0.099/kWh," described as an operating-cost/PPA-style figure with 50-day shipment, not an installed CAPEX $/kW number — [Introl Blog: Fuel Cells — AI Data Center Power's $7.65B Dark Horse](https://introl.com/blog/fuel-cells-data-center-power-dark-horse-7-billion)
- Bloom Energy's early-years fuel-cell stack replacement interval was typically 12–18 months; from 2017 onward the company stated it expected to average **over five years** between replacements — [search-summarized, citing Bloom Energy Corp SEC filings and company statements](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1664703/000166470320000013/ex1036secondamendedandrest.htm)
- A contested/critical third-party investigative source (short-seller report) claims field data suggests actual stack life closer to roughly two years, disputing Bloom's stated multi-year average — [Hindenburg Research: Bloom Energy — A "Clean" Energy Darling Wilting to its Demise](https://hindenburgresearch.com/bloom-energy-a-clean-energy-darling-wilting-to-its-demise/); also see [HNTRBRK: Do Bloom's Fuel Cells Work as Claimed? Data Says No](https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/bloom-2) — **both sources are adversarial (short-seller/investigative) and not vendor- or regulator-verified; present as a disputed claim, not fact.**
- Bloom's standard customer contracts include an Output Guaranty of 95% (measured annually) and an Efficiency Guaranty of 52% (measured cumulatively), with a stated design intent that aggregate parts replacement value within 5 years of installation stays below 80% of total system value — [SEC EDGAR: Bloom Energy Corp 8-K exhibit, FY2021](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1664703/000119312521051189/d24355dex991.htm)
- Bloom Energy's accrued warranty liability rose from $20.0 million (Dec 31, 2025) to $77.8 million (June 30, 2026), which the company attributes to "the effects of fleet degradation" — [search-summarized from recent Bloom Energy SEC filing commentary](https://hntrbrk.com/investigations/bloom-2) — an indicator that degradation-related costs are material and rising, though the underlying per-unit degradation rate was not itself sourced.

### Inferences
- No vendor has published a current (2024–2026), primary-source, system-level $/kW installed cost figure discoverable in this search pass; any CAPEX figure used in the MYSGH2 project's economic modeling should be treated as an assumption (per CLAUDE.md No-Fabrication Rule) until a current, primary vendor/investor-relations figure is located and cited.
- The rising warranty liability trend at Bloom (2025→2026) combined with the disputed short-seller claims suggests stack degradation and replacement economics remain a live commercial risk area for SOFC fleets generally, not just Bloom — worth flagging for any techno-economic comparison against alternative power sources in this project.

### Gaps
- No footprint (m²/MW or ft²/MW) data was found for any vendor other than Bloom, and even the Bloom figure is not confirmed against a primary datasheet (the primary PDF fetch was blocked by network egress policy in this session) — needs re-verification.
- No current (2024–2026) installed CAPEX $/kW figure was found for any OEM; all CAPEX data points found are either historical (2016–2018 Bloom S-1 filings) or an operating-cost ($/kWh) rather than capital-cost figure.
- No stack degradation rate (%/year or %/1000h) or replacement-interval data were found for Mitsubishi Power, Ceres/licensees, Convion, SolydEra, Sunfire, or Toshiba.

## Commercial deployment track record: reference plants, notable customers, APAC/Singapore/Malaysia installations

### Takeaway
Bloom Energy has the deepest and most APAC-relevant commercial track record among all SOFC OEMs reviewed: a multi-hundred-MW cumulative sales partnership with SK ecoplant in South Korea, a 10 MW deployment in Taiwan, and — most directly relevant to this project — active 2024–2025 commercial activity in Singapore (a data-center fuel-cell expansion and a February 2025 Sembcorp partnership tied to Singapore's Green Data Centre Roadmap, plus a DayOne AI data-centre project with an on-site 0.3 MW SOFC proof-of-concept). Mitsubishi Power's SOFC-hybrid deployment history found is limited to a single Tokyo (Marunouchi Building) installation from ~2019 at sub-MW scale; no Malaysia deployments were found for any vendor in this search pass. Ceres Power's commercial track record is measured through its licensees' manufacturing scale-up (Doosan's 50 MW/year Korean factory, Weichai's planned China facility) rather than through deployed reference power plants of its own.

## Commercial/maturity status and TRL for hydrogen-fueled vs. NG-fueled SOFC operation

### Takeaway
The large majority of currently operating commercial SOFC fleets (Bloom's installed base, Mitsubishi Power's Tokyo installation, Convion's and SolydEra's deployed CHP units) run on natural gas or biogas today; pure-hydrogen or hydrogen-blend operation exists as a discrete, separately-specified commercial product (Bloom) or as a pilot/demonstration-stage configuration (Convion C60, DayOne Singapore, SK ecoplant/Jeju Korea) rather than as the default fleet-wide fuel. Bloom Energy's pure-hydrogen product is the most commercially mature hydrogen-fueled SOFC offering found, with a published data sheet (implying it is sold, not just demonstrated), but no large-scale (multi-MW), long-duration, third-party-verified pure-hydrogen commercial operating history was found in this search — the DayOne Singapore project (0.3 MW) and Jeju Korea SOEC project (1.8 MW, and note SOEC is electrolysis not power generation) are the most concrete near-term hydrogen-linked commercial commitments identified, and both are explicitly framed as pilots/proofs-of-concept, not mature fleet-wide operation.

### Cited Findings
- Bloom Energy Servers are fuel-flexible across natural gas, biogas, blended hydrogen, or pure hydrogen, with a dedicated Hydrogen Data Sheet product specification (≥99.90% purity hydrogen, 300 kW+, ~52% average / 60% initial-maximum efficiency) — indicating a productized, sold offering rather than a lab-only demonstration — [Bloom Energy Hydrogen Data Sheet](https://www.bloomenergy.com/resource/hydrogen-spec-sheet/); [Bloom Energy Announces Hydrogen-Powered Energy Servers](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-announces-hydrogen-powered-energy-servers-to-make-always-on-renewable-electricity-a-reality/)
- Convion's C60 can be configured for natural gas, biogas, or hydrogen fuel-gas compositions, indicating hydrogen operation is available as a configuration option on an existing commercial CHP product rather than the standard/default fuel — [Elcogen: Convion brings its C60 fuel cell system to market](https://elcogen.com/elcogens-partner-convion-brings-its-c60-fuel-cell-system-to-market/)
- DayOne's Singapore data-centre SOFC installation (0.3 MW) is explicitly described as "a proof-of-concept for hydrogen-based energy," indicating pilot/demonstration status rather than mature commercial fleet operation as of the 2025 groundbreaking — [Hydrogen Central: DayOne breaks ground](https://hydrogen-central.com/dayone-breaks-ground-on-green-ai-data-centre-in-singapore-20mw-site-will-trial-solid-oxide-fuel-cell-sofc-hydrogen-power/)
- The Bloom/SK ecoplant/Korea Southern Power Jeju Island project (1.8 MW SOEC, commencing late 2025) is described as "a first-of-its-kind demonstration for South Korea" — i.e., pilot/demonstration status, and note this is solid oxide **electrolysis** (hydrogen production), not SOFC power generation, so it evidences Bloom's adjacent SOEC maturity rather than hydrogen-fueled SOFC power generation specifically — [Bloom Energy and SK ecoplant Collaborate on a Major Hydrogen Project](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-and-sk-ecoplant-collaborate-on-a-major-hydrogen-project-to-be-developed-by-korea-southern-power-co-ltd/)
- Mitsubishi Power's demonstrated SOFC-MGT hybrid operation (4,000-hour continuous test, Marunouchi Building installation) was run on hydrocarbon fuel; no hydrogen-fueled Mitsubishi Power SOFC-hybrid operation was found in this search pass — [MHI: 4,000-Hour Continuous Operation of Pressurized SOFC-MGT Hybrid](https://www.mhi.com/news/1309201714.html) — absence noted, not confirmed as impossible; MHI's separate SOEC (electrolysis) test module at Takasago Hydrogen Park (400 kW, began April 2024) is a hydrogen-production technology, not hydrogen-fueled SOFC power generation — [MHI Begins Operation of SOEC Test Module at Takasago Hydrogen Park](https://power.mhi.com/news/240425.html)

### Inferences
- Across the vendors surveyed, hydrogen-fueled SOFC power generation is best characterized as commercially available as a specified product option (Bloom) or an engineering configuration option (Convion), but validated in the field mostly at sub-MW pilot scale (DayOne Singapore 0.3 MW) rather than at the multi-hundred-MW fleet scale that natural-gas-fueled SOFC has achieved (Bloom's 400 MW+ Korea fleet, for example, is presumed natural-gas-fueled based on the sources found, though this was not explicitly confirmed per-site).
- For the MYSGH2 project's H2-pipeline-to-Singapore offtake context, if SOFC is being considered as a downstream hydrogen-consuming power-generation technology, Bloom Energy's pure-hydrogen product line is the most commercially mature candidate found, but its field-proven scale on pure H₂ remains limited (pilot-scale) as of this research pass, and long-duration/large-scale independent verification of the pure-hydrogen efficiency and degradation claims was not found.

### Gaps
- No independently verified (non-vendor) long-duration hydrogen-fueled SOFC operating data (efficiency over time, degradation rate on hydrogen specifically) was found for any vendor — all hydrogen-specific efficiency and status claims found trace back to vendor press releases/datasheets.
- No explicit TRL (Technology Readiness Level) number was found stated by any vendor or independent body for hydrogen-fueled vs. natural-gas-fueled SOFC operation; maturity assessments above are qualitative inferences from deployment/pilot status language in the sources, not a cited TRL figure — this should be flagged as an assumption-level qualitative judgment if used in the final report, per the project's No-Fabrication Rule.
- No DOE/NREL/EPRI technology-readiness report was retrieved in this search pass (the assignment's suggested source category was not reached within the tool-call budget) — recommend a follow-up search specifically against DOE Hydrogen Program / NREL / EPRI SOFC technology reports if this level of rigor is required downstream.
