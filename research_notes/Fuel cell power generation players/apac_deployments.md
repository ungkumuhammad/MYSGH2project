# Commercial-Scale (MW-Class) Stationary Fuel Cell Power Deployments in Asia-Pacific

Research date: 2026-09-22. All figures below carry their source; where a figure could not be confirmed this is stated explicitly rather than estimated.

## South Korea: scale of the stationary fuel cell fleet and named reference plants

### Takeaway
South Korea has by far the world's largest stationary fuel cell fleet, built almost entirely on natural-gas-fed PAFC/MCFC technology under the Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS), with installed capacity reported at roughly 800 MW–1 GW+ as of 2025 and a government target of 15,000 MW by 2040. Only one confirmed plant (Hanwha/Doosan's Daesan facility, 50 MW) is genuinely fed by hydrogen (by-product hydrogen from a petrochemical complex, not imported/green hydrogen) rather than natural gas reformed on-site; most other "hydrogen fuel cell power plant" branding in Korean press refers to PAFC/MCFC units that internally reform natural gas to hydrogen, not externally supplied hydrogen.

### Cited Findings
- Doosan Fuel Cell has supplied a cumulative total of 1,197 units (526.71 MW) in Korea including PAFC, and was installing a further 390 units (171.16 MW) as of the reporting date — [Enki.AI, "Doosan Fuel Cell SOFC: Inside the 2025 Strategic Pivot"](https://enkiai.com/fuel-cells/doosan-fuel-cell-sofc-inside-the-2025-strategic-pivot/) (note: secondary/aggregator source, not a Doosan primary filing; treat capacity figure as approximate).
- Between 2021–2024, Doosan Fuel Cell led Korea's stationary fuel cell market with large-scale PAFC projects including the Shinincheon and Pohang power plants — [Enki.AI, "Doosan Fuel Cell SOFC: Inside the 2025 Strategic Pivot"](https://enkiai.com/fuel-cells/doosan-fuel-cell-sofc-inside-the-2025-strategic-pivot/).
- In July 2025 Doosan began mass production at a new 50 MW/yr SOFC factory in Jeollabuk-do, using technology licensed from UK's Ceres Power — [Enki.AI, "Doosan Fuel Cell SOFC: Inside the 2025 Strategic Pivot"](https://enkiai.com/fuel-cells/doosan-fuel-cell-sofc-inside-the-2025-strategic-pivot/).
- A 360 MW fuel cell facility (POSCO Energy / Doosan-supplied) was described as the world's largest, planned to enter operation in 2018 in Pyeongtaek City south of Seoul — [Industrial Info, "World's Largest Fuel Cell Power Park to Be Built in South Korea"](https://www.industrialinfo.com/news/article/worlds-largest-fuel-cell-power-park-to-be-built-in-south-korea--230250) (a planning-stage figure; not confirmed here as actually commissioned at 360 MW — see Gaps).
- **Gyeonggi Green Energy fuel cell park, Hwasung City** — 59 MW, comprising 21 × FuelCell Energy DFC3000 (2.8 MW each, MCFC technology), built by POSCO Energy (construction began Nov 2012, commercial operation from 19 Feb 2014), fuelled by **natural gas** (NG-reformed, not externally supplied hydrogen), providing combined heat and power (district heating) to the grid; ownership: POSCO Energy 15%, Samchully 15%, Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP, a KEPCO subsidiary) 49% — [PowerMag, "59-MW Fuel Cell Park Opening Heralds Robust Global Technology Future"](https://www.powermag.com/59-mw-fuel-cell-park-opening-heralds-robust-global-technology-future/); [GlobeNewswire/FuelCell Energy, "World's Largest Fuel Cell Park Completed in South Korea" (19 Feb 2014)](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2014/02/19/611481/10068981/en/World-s-Largest-Fuel-Cell-Park-Completed-in-South-Korea.html).
- **Shinincheon Bitdream Fuel Cell Power Plant, Incheon** — 78.96 MW total capacity, built in four stages since 2017 using PAFC/fuel-cell units supplied by POSCO Energy and Doosan Fuel Cell; commissioned/opened around October 2021; described in Korean/Indian press as the "world's largest hydrogen fuel cell power plant," intended to supply electricity to ~250,000 households and heat to 44,000 more — [H2 View, "New 78.96MW hydrogen fuel cell power plant opens in South Korea"](https://www.h2-view.com/story/new-78-96mw-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant-opens-in-south-korea/); [ANI News, "World's largest hydrogen fuel cell power plant was built in Korea" (Oct 2021)](https://www.aninews.in/news/world/asia/worlds-largest-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant-was-built-in-korea20211027162812/); [Doosan Fuel Cell press release](https://www.doosanfuelcell.com/en/media-center/medi-0101_view/?id=57); [FuelCellsWorks, "World's Largest Hydrogen Fuel Cell Power Plant Jointly Built by Doosan Fuel Cell Put Into Service"](https://fuelcellsworks.com/news/worlds-largest-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant-jointly-built-by-doosan-fuel-cell-put-into-service).
  - **Fuel source caveat:** none of the sources found in this search specify that Shinincheon Bitdream is fed by an external/imported hydrogen supply; Doosan's own PAFC product line is described elsewhere as available in hydrogen, natural-gas (NG), and dual-fuel (LPG/NG) variants, and Korean press commonly labels NG-reformed PAFC/MCFC plants "hydrogen fuel cell power plants" because the electrochemical reaction itself runs on hydrogen extracted on-site from natural gas — [Doosan Mobility Innovation, "SOFC NG" product page](https://www.doosanmobility.com/en/products/sofc-ng"). Treat "hydrogen fuel cell" branding for Shinincheon as **NG-fed unless a primary source confirms otherwise** — this could not be confirmed either way in this search (see Gaps).
- **Daesan Hydrogen Fuel Cell Power Plant (Hanwha Energy / Hanwha Engineering & Construction, with Doosan-supplied fuel cells)** — 50 MW, described as the world's first and largest fuel cell power plant running on **by-product hydrogen** (from the Daesan petrochemical/naphtha-cracking complex), started commercial operation June 2020 (completion ceremony 28 July 2020); annual output ~400,000 MWh, powering an estimated ~160,000 households; Doosan supplied 114 × 440 kW hydrogen fuel cell units; total project cost ~US$212 million — [PowerMag, "Innovative Byproduct-Hydrogen Fuel Cell Power Plant Completed"](https://www.powermag.com/innovative-byproduct-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant-completed/); [Robotics & Automation News, citing Hanwha Energy (21 Aug 2020)](https://roboticsandautomationnews.com/2020/08/21/hanwha-energy-celebrates-completion-of-worlds-first-and-largest-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant/35447/); [FuelCellsWorks, "South Korea: Work Begins on World's Largest Hydrogen Fuel Cell Power Plant"](https://fuelcellsworks.com/news/south-korea-work-begins-on-worlds-largest-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant-commissioned). This is the **clearest confirmed example of a genuinely hydrogen-fed (not NG-fed) MW-scale plant in Korea**, though the hydrogen is industrial by-product hydrogen, not green/imported hydrogen.
- **Hadong** — searched specifically; no plant by this name was found in any source returned; likely conflated with Daesan or another Korean site. Treat as **not confirmed / not found**.
- A 100 MW fuel cell BOP (balance-of-plant) manufacturing factory was completed at Yeonsan, Pohang in September 2008, operated by POSCO Energy — this is a manufacturing facility, not a power-generation plant — [search result summary citing POSCO Energy sources; primary URL not independently verified in this pass].
- South Korea's installed stationary fuel cell capacity is reported as exceeding 800 MW in 2025, with growth to 150–200 MW/yr currently and 250–350 MW/yr by 2030, and a government Hydrogen Economy Roadmap (2019) target of 15,000 MW of stationary fuel cells by 2040 — figures drawn from an aggregator search summary; **not independently verified against a primary Korea Energy Agency or MOTIE document in this pass** — flagged as a claim needing primary-source confirmation. [Search-aggregated; primary source not directly fetched — see Gaps.]
- Separately, "South Korea leads with over 1 GW (1,000 MW) of installed stationary fuel cell capacity" was also returned by search as a competing figure to the ~800 MW figure above — the two numbers are **not reconciled** and may reflect different report vintages; flagged as a conflict. [Search-aggregated summaries; primary sources not directly fetched.]

### Inferences
- Korea's fleet is overwhelmingly natural-gas-fed PAFC/MCFC under RPS incentives (REC weighting), not hydrogen-import-fed generation; only Daesan (50 MW, by-product hydrogen) is a clear exception, and it uses industrial by-product hydrogen rather than imported/green ammonia-derived hydrogen — meaning even Korea's "hydrogen fuel cell" fleet is not directly comparable in feedstock logistics to this project's green-ammonia-import concept.
- The "world's largest hydrogen fuel cell power plant" branding used for Shinincheon Bitdream in press coverage appears to be marketing/translation shorthand for a PAFC plant (fuel cells run on hydrogen internally, sourced by on-site NG reforming) rather than a plant supplied with pure external hydrogen — this distinction is important for the report writer to flag to avoid overstating Korea's genuinely hydrogen-fed capacity.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm from a primary Doosan/POSCO source whether Shinincheon Bitdream's individual units run on natural gas (internally reformed) or on delivered hydrogen; H2 View, Doosan and FuelCellsWorks press articles were returned only as search snippets (h2-view.com and hanwha.com were blocked by network egress restrictions in this session and could not be fetched directly for full text).
- The 360 MW Pyeongtaek "world's largest fuel cell power park" project (2014 ScienceDirect reference, industrialinfo.com 2013/2014 article) — could not confirm whether this was actually built at 360 MW or scaled back; treat as an early-2010s plan, not a confirmed as-built capacity.
- Total current (2025/2026) Korean stationary fuel cell fleet capacity is reported inconsistently across secondary sources (~800 MW vs. >1,000 MW) — no primary Korea Energy Agency, MOTIE, or KEPCO statistical release was directly retrieved to reconcile this; report writer should treat any single total-fleet figure as approximate pending a primary-source check.
- No figure for "Pohang power plant" capacity (referenced by name in the Doosan 2021-2024 project list) could be separated from the unrelated 100 MW Pohang BOP manufacturing factory; these appear to be two different Pohang-area facilities and this could not be disambiguated with confidence.

## Japan: utility-scale stationary fuel cell projects and hydrogen-specific pilots

### Takeaway
Japan's fuel cell landscape beyond residential ENE-FARM units is dominated by hydrogen *production* R&D and small/pilot-scale hydrogen power demonstrations rather than MW-class grid power generation from stationary fuel cells; no evidence was found of a Korea-scale (tens-of-MW) hydrogen-fed or NG-fed stationary fuel cell power plant in Japan.

### Cited Findings
- The Fukushima Hydrogen Energy Research Field (FH2R) in Namie, Fukushima Prefecture — developed by NEDO, Toshiba Energy Systems & Solutions, Tohoku Electric Power, and Iwatani — is a 10 MW-class renewable-hydrogen **production** facility (water electrolysis powered by a 20 MW co-located photovoltaic farm), completed end-February 2020, capable of producing up to 1,200 Nm³/h of hydrogen; construction began 2018 — [NEDO press release](https://www.nedo.go.jp/english/news/AA5en_100393.html); [Toshiba Energy Systems & Solutions news release](https://www.global.toshiba/ww/news/energy/2020/03/news-20200307-01.html). This is a hydrogen production/demonstration site, not a stationary fuel cell power plant; hydrogen produced there is used for stationary fuel cell systems, mobility (FCVs, buses), and other end-uses, per NEDO/Toshiba materials.
- Toshiba Energy Systems & Solutions announced it would supply its H2Rex™ fuel cell system to the Tanaka Shonan Plant, per a March 2024 Toshiba press release — [Toshiba, "Toshiba to Supply H2Rex™ Fuel Cell System to Tanaka Shonan Plant" (13 Mar 2024)](https://www.global.toshiba/ww/news/energy/2024/03/news-20240313-01.html). Capacity/MW scale and fuel source (hydrogen vs. NG) for this specific installation were **not captured** in the search snippet and were not independently fetched in this pass — flagged as a gap.
- No Kobe-specific hydrogen fuel cell power project was returned in search results; only Fukushima (Namie) and the Tanaka Shonan Plant (location/prefecture not confirmed) appeared as Japan-specific hydrogen/fuel-cell power items.

### Inferences
- Japan's national hydrogen strategy emphasis to date (in the material surfaced here) skews toward hydrogen production and mobility/fuel-cell-vehicle demonstration rather than grid-scale stationary fuel cell power generation — a contrast with Korea's RPS-driven build-out of tens-of-MW stationary fuel cell parks.

### Gaps
- Could not identify any Japanese utility-scale (MW-class) stationary fuel cell power plant analogous to Korea's Shinincheon/Daesan/Gyeonggi facilities; this may reflect a genuine absence of such projects, or a limitation of the searches run (Kobe-specific and utility-company-led projects, e.g. from Kansai Electric, JERA, or Osaka Gas, were not directly searched and should be checked in a follow-up pass if Japan detail is needed).
- Capacity (MW), fuel type, and status of the Toshiba H2Rex Tanaka Shonan Plant project could not be confirmed beyond the announcement headline; primary release not fetched.

## Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia: fuel cell power generation projects, pilots, or announcements

### Takeaway
Taiwan and Singapore each have at least one confirmed commercial fuel cell deployment (both Bloom Energy solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) installations, both natural-gas-fed for behind-the-meter/data-center or industrial power, not grid-connected hydrogen-fed plants); no MW-scale fuel cell power generation project (built, under construction, or FID'd) was found for Malaysia as of the 2026-09-22 research date — Malaysia's hydrogen-sector activity found in this search is limited to hydrogen production/refuelling-station pilots for mobility, not stationary power generation via fuel cells.

### Cited Findings
- **Taiwan — Unimicron (Bloom Energy SOFC).** Bloom Energy agreed to deploy up to 10 MW of solid oxide fuel cells across Unimicron (chip-substrate/PCB maker) plants in Taiwan; first phase (600 kW Energy Server) installed August 2023, this being Bloom's first installation in Taiwan; full 10 MW contract scheduled for completion by 2026; fuel source is natural gas (via Taiwan's underground gas pipeline network, per Bloom's own description of how its systems avoid weather/grid vulnerability) — [Bloom Energy, "Bloom Energy Installs First Fuel Cells in Taiwan"](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-installs-first-fuel-cells-in-taiwan/); [Hart Energy, "Bloom Energy Installs First Fuel Cells in Taiwan"](https://www.hartenergy.com/exclusives/bloom-energy-installs-first-fuel-cells-taiwan-206191/); [Bloom Energy investor press release](https://investor.bloomenergy.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2023/Bloom-Energy-Installs-First-Fuel-Cells-in-Taiwan/default.aspx).
- **Singapore — SK ecoplant / Bloom Energy for GDS data center.** SK ecoplant (Korea), a long-standing Bloom Energy partner, will deploy Bloom fuel cells at a new Singapore data center for GDS (China's largest data-center developer); this is described as Bloom's first project in Singapore. The project sits within a wider Singapore Economic Development Board / Infocomm Media Development Authority allocation of up to ~80 MW of new data-center ICT capacity shared among four operators (GDS among them) — the 80 MW figure is total allocated data-center capacity, **not** a confirmed fuel-cell-specific MW figure; the fuel-cell portion's own MW rating was not stated in the source retrieved — [Bloom Energy, "Singapore Data Center to Expand Capacity with Bloom Energy Fuel Cells"](https://www.bloomenergy.com/blog/singapore-data-center-to-expand-capacity-with-bloom-energy-fuel-cells/). Bloom's fuel cells are described generically (on this Singapore project) as running on natural gas, biogas, or hydrogen, but the Singapore project is not confirmed here as hydrogen-fed — treat as NG-fed by default absent confirmation.
- **Singapore — Tuas Power 670 MW hydrogen-ready gas plant.** Tuas Power won rights from EMA to build a 670 MW hydrogen-ready gas turbine (combined-cycle) plant capable of burning up to 30% hydrogen by volume, targeted operational by 2031 — [FuelCellsWorks, "Singapore Bets on Hydrogen-Ready Gas: Tuas Power Wins Rights to a 670 MW Plant That Can Burn 30 Percent Hydrogen by 2031" (20 Aug 2026)](https://fuelcellsworks.com/2026/08/20/clean-energy/singapore-bets-on-hydrogen-ready-gas-tuas-power-wins-rights-to-a-670-mw-plant-that-can-burn-30-percent-hydrogen-by-2031). **This is a gas turbine plant, not a fuel cell plant** — included here for completeness/context because it is Singapore's most significant hydrogen-capable grid-power commitment, but it must not be mischaracterized as a fuel cell deployment.
- **Malaysia — no MW-scale fuel cell power generation project found.** Malaysia's hydrogen-related activity surfaced in this search consists of: (a) Sarawak Energy + Linde EOX-built hydrogen production/refuelling station supporting 3 hydrogen fuel-cell buses and 2 fuel-cell cars (mobility, not stationary power) — [ASEAN Centre for Energy, citing Sarawak Energy](https://aseanenergy.org/news-clipping/sarawak-cm-launches-south-east-asias-first-integrated-hydrogen-production-plant/); (b) Sarawak Energy–Petronas MoU (2020) on hydrogen production exploration, and Petronas/SEDC Energy collaboration on green hydrogen via PEM electrolysis for refuelling stations — [Sarawak Energy press release](https://www.sarawakenergy.com/media-info/media-releases/2020/sarawak-energy-petronas-ink-mou-for-greater-collaboration-in-hydrogen-production-exploration); [Petronas Global, "PETRONAS, SEDC Energy Collaborate to Spur Adoption of Hydrogen, Renewable Oil in Sarawak"](https://www.petronas.com/media/media-releases/petronas-sedc-energy-collaborate-spur-adoption-hydrogen-renewable-oil-sarawak); (c) Sarawak government plans for three "catalytic" green hydrogen projects — one in Kuching for domestic use (targeted completion 2025) and two in Bintulu for export (targeted 2027) — [Asia Pacific Green Hydrogen, "Energy: Can Malaysia be a hydrogen hub?"](https://hydrogenapac.com/2024/07/18/energy-can-malaysia-be-a-hydrogen-hub/). None of these are described as stationary fuel cell power-generation plants; they are production/export/mobility-refuelling projects. **Explicitly: no MW-scale fuel cell power generation project was found in Malaysia as of this research date (2026-09-22).**

### Inferences
- Singapore's and Taiwan's fuel cell deployments to date are commercial/industrial behind-the-meter power (data centers, PCB manufacturing), both NG-fed SOFC from Bloom Energy — a fundamentally different use case from grid-scale hydrogen-import power generation.
- Malaysia's hydrogen strategy (at least as surfaced here) is oriented toward green hydrogen/ammonia **production and export** (e.g., Sarawak/Bintulu) and domestic mobility refuelling, not toward hydrogen power generation via fuel cells — consistent with Malaysia's role in this project as an import/cracking site rather than a fuel-cell power market in its own right.

### Gaps
- Fuel-cell-specific MW rating for the SK ecoplant/Bloom Energy GDS Singapore data-center project was not stated in the source retrieved (only the broader ~80 MW EDB/IMDA data-center capacity allocation was found) — needs a primary Bloom Energy or SK ecoplant release with the fuel-cell nameplate MW to confirm.
- No search was run specifically for Singapore government (EMA) fuel-cell-specific policy or subsidy schemes (distinct from its broader hydrogen strategy, covered below) — if the report writer needs this, a follow-up search on "EMA Singapore fuel cell grant/pilot" is recommended.

## Hydrogen-fed (not NG-fed) fuel cell projects/FEED studies in Asia comparable to this project's ambition

### Takeaway
The clearest confirmed genuinely-hydrogen-fed MW-scale fuel cell power plant found anywhere in Asia-Pacific is Korea's Daesan by-product-hydrogen plant (50 MW); no project or FEED study was found anywhere in the region proposing fuel cells fed by **imported** hydrogen (e.g., ammonia-cracked, shipped-in hydrogen) for grid power at a scale/ambition comparable to this project's Johor-to-Singapore concept — the closest comparator found is Singapore's Tuas Power 670 MW hydrogen-ready **gas turbine** (not fuel cell) project, and a general (non-fuel-cell) 200 MW hydrogen-fired power station reference in China.

### Cited Findings
- Korea's Daesan plant (50 MW, Hanwha/Doosan, by-product hydrogen from a petrochemical complex) remains the only clearly-confirmed hydrogen-fed (as opposed to NG-reformed) MW-scale stationary fuel cell plant identified in this research — see Korea section above for full sourcing.
- A search result referenced "a project in China [that] includes a 200 MW hydrogen-fired power station for grid backup and six hydrogen filling stations designed to fuel 600 trucks" — this was returned only as an unattributed aggregator summary with no direct primary source URL captured, and it is unclear whether this is a fuel cell plant or a hydrogen-combustion turbine/engine plant. **Treat as unconfirmed / needs primary sourcing** — [search-aggregated summary; primary source not identified in this pass].
- Singapore's Tuas Power 670 MW hydrogen-ready plant (targeted 2031, up to 30% hydrogen blend) is a gas turbine combined-cycle plant, not a fuel cell, and is a blending (≤30%) design, not a pure-hydrogen design — [FuelCellsWorks (20 Aug 2026)](https://fuelcellsworks.com/2026/08/20/clean-energy/singapore-bets-on-hydrogen-ready-gas-tuas-power-wins-rights-to-a-670-mw-plant-that-can-burn-30-percent-hydrogen-by-2031). Its ≤30% blend design is well below the near-100% hydrogen implied by this project's ammonia-crack-to-H2-pipeline concept, and ASME B31.12 (cited in this project's CLAUDE.md) applies specifically above 10% molar H2 — relevant context for comparing regulatory regimes, though Tuas Power's project is combustion turbine technology, not electrochemical fuel cell technology.
- Korea's Hydrogen Economy Roadmap (2019) and Japan's Basic Hydrogen Strategy (2017) are both cited (in an aggregator source) as emphasizing significant future demand for **imported** hydrogen into East Asia and Korea's ambition to build a global hydrogen import supply chain — [aggregator search summary]; this is stated at a strategy level and not tied to any specific named fuel-cell FEED study — see Gaps.

### Inferences
- No evidence was found of any Asia-Pacific project or FEED study that combines (a) imported hydrogen/ammonia as feedstock and (b) fuel cell (rather than gas-turbine/combustion) technology for grid-scale power at MW-class scale — this project's concept (ammonia import → on-site cracking → piped H2 → Singapore grid, evaluated for fuel-cell or other end-use power generation) therefore appears to have **no direct precedent** yet built or FID'd in the region among the sources surfaced here. This is a notable finding for the report writer: the project sits ahead of, not alongside, confirmed regional practice in combining imported-hydrogen feedstock with fuel cell power generation at grid scale.

### Gaps
- Could not locate a named FEED study anywhere in Asia specifically proposing hydrogen-fed fuel cells (as opposed to hydrogen-fed or hydrogen-blended gas turbines) for grid power — this may be because none exists yet, or because the searches run (general web search only, ~15 tool calls) did not surface trade-press/analyst-report coverage of an early-stage FEED that has not yet been publicly announced. A follow-up search specifically on "Ammonia Energy Association hydrogen fuel cell FEED Asia" or IEA Hydrogen Project database entries (not directly queried in this pass) is recommended if this question is load-bearing for the report.
- The China 200 MW "hydrogen-fired power station" reference needs primary sourcing and technology-type confirmation (fuel cell vs. combustion) before it can be cited with confidence.

## Government policy support for stationary fuel cells (Korea/Japan)

### Takeaway
Korea's stationary fuel cell market size is directly attributable to its Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS, since 2012), which gives fuel cells a REC (Renewable Energy Certificate) weighting of 2 — higher than several other renewable technologies — creating a strong economic incentive for power producers with >500 MW capacity to deploy fuel cells to meet their renewable obligation; this policy framework is now being restructured, with hydrogen/fuel-cell provisions being moved to a dedicated Hydrogen Economy Promotion and Hydrogen Safety Management Act as of a March 2026 amendment. No Japan-specific stationary-fuel-cell subsidy mechanism (distinct from the well-known residential ENE-FARM subsidy program) was identified in this research pass.

### Cited Findings
- Korea's RPS system, in place since 2012, requires power generation companies with more than 500 MW of installed capacity to source a mandatory share of total generation from renewables; the required ratio began at 2% and has expanded to 15% "this year" (source's reference year), with a statutory cap of 25% — [IEA, "Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS) – Policies"](https://www.iea.org/policies/4837-renewable-portfolio-standard-rps); corroborated by [Chambers and Partners, "Renewable Energy 2025 - South Korea"](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/renewable-energy-2025/south-korea).
- Under RPS, fuel cells receive a REC weighting of 2 (per MWh generated), higher than onshore wind, incentivizing fuel cell deployment specifically among eligible technologies — [search-aggregated summary of RPS technology weightings; primary MOTIE/KEA weighting table not directly fetched in this pass — see Gaps].
- In March 2026, South Korea revised the underlying renewable energy act (renamed the Act on the Promotion of the Development, Use, and Diffusion of Renewable Energy), and as part of that amendment, provisions relating to "new energy" sources including hydrogen and fuel cells were transferred to the Hydrogen Economy Promotion and Hydrogen Safety Management Act — [search-aggregated summary; primary legislative text not directly fetched — see Gaps].
- Korea's government released a Hydrogen Economy Roadmap in 2019 setting a target of 15,000 MW of stationary fuel cells by 2040 — [search-aggregated summary; see Korea section above for the same figure, primary source (MOTIE) not directly fetched in this pass].
- South Korea is separately reported to be advancing a bill to scrap the renewable quota (RPS) system altogether — [pv magazine, "South Korea advances bill to scrap renewable quota system" (20 May 2026)](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/05/20/south-korea-advances-bill-to-scrap-renewable-quota-system/). This is a significant and recent (mid-2026) development: if RPS is scrapped, the REC-weighting mechanism that has driven Korea's fuel cell build-out could change materially — **flagged as a live policy risk/uncertainty relevant to forecasting future Korean fuel cell deployment**, and the report writer should treat any forward-looking Korean fuel-cell-market projection with this caveat.

### Inferences
- Korea's fuel cell market leadership is a direct, traceable product of RPS REC-weighting policy rather than organic market economics; the May 2026 move to scrap RPS (if it proceeds) introduces material uncertainty into whether Korea's historical growth rate in fuel cell deployment will continue, which is relevant context for anyone using Korea as a benchmark for fuel-cell-driven hydrogen offtake economics in this project's own Singapore power analysis.

### Gaps
- No Japan-specific stationary/utility-scale fuel cell subsidy program (paralleling Korea's RPS/REC mechanism) was identified in this research pass; only the residential ENE-FARM subsidy is well known from general knowledge and was not the focus of search here (explicitly excluded by the task scope, which asked about non-residential/utility-scale support). If Japan's policy detail becomes load-bearing, a dedicated search on METI/NEDO hydrogen power generation subsidy programs is recommended.
- The exact REC weighting table (all technologies, not just fuel cells vs. onshore wind) and the primary Korean statute/regulation text were not directly fetched — the "weighting of 2" figure should be treated as reported-but-not-primary-verified.
- Could not confirm the practical impact (if any already visible) of the March 2026 hydrogen/fuel-cell provision transfer to the new Hydrogen Economy Promotion and Hydrogen Safety Management Act, nor the current (September 2026) status of the RPS-scrapping bill reported by pv magazine in May 2026.
