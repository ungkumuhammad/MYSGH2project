# Stationary Fuel Cell vs. Gas Turbine/Reciprocating Engine Benchmarks (Footprint, CAPEX, Efficiency, Maturity)

> Reference comparators already established in this project (not restated as new findings below): gas turbine CCGT footprint ~0.111–0.139 acres/MW (~450–563 m²/MW); gas turbine/engine simple-cycle efficiencies ~35–46% LHV.

## What land footprint (m²/MW or acres/MW) do fuel cell power plants require, across PAFC/MCFC/SOFC/PEMFC? Are fuel cells generally more compact than gas turbines per MW?

### Takeaway
Sourced, project-level footprint data for fuel cells is sparse and inconsistent in how it is measured (nameplate site footprint vs. cell-stack power density vs. marketing claims). The one concrete multi-MW MCFC park figure found (~0.088 acres/MW) is in the same order of magnitude as, or somewhat more compact than, this project's gas-turbine CCGT reference (~0.111–0.139 acres/MW), but the underlying scope (whether it includes balance-of-plant, parking, setbacks, etc.) could not be verified, so treat this as directional, not a rigorously apples-to-apples comparison. Independent NREL/DOE siting studies that directly compare fuel cell vs. gas turbine footprint on a like-for-like basis were not found.

### Cited Findings
- A commercial MCFC installation (fuel cell "park") of 59 MW required approximately 5.2 acres of site area — ≈0.088 acres/MW (≈356 m²/MW) — per a search-aggregated citation traced to FuelCell Energy's own materials; original primary document not independently retrieved in this pass, so treat as a vendor-linked figure pending direct-source confirmation. — [FuelCell Energy blog: Repeatable Blocks: Scalability Advantages of Fuel Cells](https://www.fuelcellenergy.com/blog/fuel-cell-scalability)
- EPA fact sheet material (search-indexed but not independently fetched due to network egress restrictions in this session) references fuel cell systems as compact/modular, suitable for space-constrained sites, without a specific m²/MW figure retrieved. — [EPA: Auxiliary and Supplemental Power Fact Sheet: Fuel Cells](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2019-08/documents/fuel_cells_fact_sheet_p1004xfm.pdf) (not directly fetched — EPA domain blocked by this session's egress proxy; content not verified beyond search snippet)
- Typical individual fuel cell unit/module sizes cited: MCFC systems 300 kW–3.7 MW per unit; PAFC systems ~400 kW per unit (e.g., Doosan PureCell-class); SOFC systems >50 kW per module (e.g., Bloom Energy Server modules, see below) — these are unit sizes, not footprint-per-MW figures, but establish that multi-MW plants are built from stacked/aggregated modules. — [DOE/NETL Fuel Cell Handbook, 7th Ed.](https://www.netl.doe.gov/sites/default/files/netl-file/FCHandbook7.pdf)
- Bloom Energy's SOFC "Energy Server" product line is a 325 kW module as of the company's most recent published datasheet (Feb 2026), with >1.5 GW deployed across 1,200+ sites globally — indicates fleet-wide modularity but no independently verified footprint-per-MW number was retrieved in this session. — [Bloom Energy Server 6.5 datasheet reference, indexed Feb 2026](https://www.bloomenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/bloom-energy-server-datasheet-feb-2026.pdf) (page not directly fetched — bloomenergy.com blocked by this session's egress proxy; content is from search snippet only, not independently verified)

### Inferences
- Because fuel cell plants are built of small modular units (hundreds of kW each) stacked in rows, footprint scales roughly linearly with capacity once site-level balance-of-plant (inverters, gas processing/cracking skids if any, cooling) is added — this is consistent with, but not proof of, the industry claim that fuel cells are more space-efficient than gas turbines, which need larger single-train equipment, stacks, and exhaust/emissions systems.
- The one concrete MCFC figure found (~0.088 acres/MW) sits within or modestly below this project's existing gas turbine CCGT range (0.111–0.139 acres/MW), which is broadly consistent with the "fuel cells are more compact" claim, but a single data point from one vendor's promotional material is not sufficient to generalize across PAFC/SOFC/PEMFC or to claim a verified independent benchmark.

### Gaps
- No independent (DOE/NREL/IEA/IRENA) siting study directly comparing fuel cell plant footprint to gas turbine plant footprint on a consistent basis (same boundary definition: nameplate footprint vs. total site incl. setbacks) was found in this session.
- No footprint figures at all were found for PAFC, SOFC, or PEMFC plants at multi-MW scale (only the MCFC figure, and that traces to a vendor source).
- Several promising primary sources (EPA fact sheet, Bloom Energy datasheets, JRC EU stationary fuel cell report, NETL/DOE PDFs) could not be directly fetched in this session because epa.gov, bloomenergy.com, and publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu are blocked by this session's network egress proxy; only search-engine snippets of these sources were available, so their content should be independently re-verified before use in a deliverable.

## What CAPEX $/kW figures exist across the fuel cell chemistries (2023–2026), and how do they compare to gas turbines and reciprocating engines?

### Takeaway
Fuel cell CAPEX figures found are dominated by EU/DOE R&D cost *targets* (not verified as-built project costs) and range roughly €5,000–7,500/kW (SOFC, micro-PEMFC) down to a DOE 2030 stationary target of $1,000/kW; one general industry rule-of-thumb cites ~$800/kW for fuel cell systems. These are broadly higher than or comparable to gas turbine and reciprocating engine benchmarks even after the 2024–2025 gas turbine price surge (turbines: ~$728–1,544/kW as of 2025 per one industry source, plus this project's existing Wood Mackenzie "+195% since 2020" figure toward ~$600/kW by 2027; reciprocating gas engines: ~$1,500/kW rule of thumb). Data quality is uneven — several figures are targets/aspirational rather than delivered-project costs, and none of the fuel cell figures found are specific to hydrogen-fueled (vs. natural-gas-fueled) systems.

### Cited Findings
- EU Clean Hydrogen Partnership presentation materials cite 2024 CAPEX **targets**: micro-SOFC 6,000 €/kW; mid/large-size SOFC 5,000 €/kW (described as "feasible" targets, not confirmed delivered costs); a specific reference data point of 50 kWel SOFC systems at 18,400 €/kW and 40 kWth systems at 16,500 €/kW (small/early-stage system, high cost). — [Clean Hydrogen Partnership: Clean Heat and Power, Kontanasiou](https://www.clean-hydrogen.europa.eu/system/files/2023-12/08_Clean%20Heat%20and%20Power_Kontanasiou.pdf)
- Same source: micro-PEMFC CAPEX target 7,500 €/kW (2020 target, i.e. already dated); large-scale PEMFC stacks aiming for CAPEX <1,500 €/kW (an aspirational target, not a confirmed achieved cost). — [Clean Hydrogen Partnership: Clean Heat and Power, Kontanasiou](https://www.clean-hydrogen.europa.eu/system/files/2023-12/08_Clean%20Heat%20and%20Power_Kontanasiou.pdf)
- DOE's Hydrogen and Fuel Cell Technologies Office has a 2030 cost target of **$1,000/kW fuel cell system cost** for stationary power applications; no explicit DOE 2024–2026 stationary fuel cell $/kW target was located in this session (only transportation and electrolyzer targets were found alongside it). — [DOE HFTO Multi-Year Program Plan references, via search](https://www.energy.gov/eere/fuelcells/hydrogen-and-fuel-cell-technologies-office-multi-year-program-plan)
- A general (non-attributed-to-a-specific-primary-source) industry estimate: "capital costs for fuel cell systems run approximately $800 per kW" — this figure's underlying source/date could not be confirmed in this session's search results and should be treated as unverified until traced to a primary document. — cited via search aggregation, no direct primary URL retrieved
- Gas turbine (peaking/CT) plants placed into service in 2025 have capital costs ranging **$728–1,544/kW**, per Gas Turbine World's cost tracking. — [Gas Turbine World: How much does it cost to build a gas power plant?](https://gasturbineworld.com/capital-costs/)
- Wood Mackenzie: gas turbine equipment prices are projected to reach ~$600/kW by end-2027, a 195% increase since 2019/2020 (baseline year varies slightly by article) due to OEM supply-demand crunch. — [Wood Mackenzie: Gas turbine prices soar 195%](https://www.woodmac.com/press-releases/gas-turbine-prices-soar-195-as-market-faces-supply-demand-crisis/); [Utility Dive: Gas turbine supply crunch set to raise prices 195% by 2027](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/gas-turbine-supply-crunch-set-to-raise-prices-195-by-2027-woodmac/816904/)
- Reciprocating gas engine capital cost rule-of-thumb: **~$1,500/kW**, cited by an energy-research newsletter analysis; capex per kW decreases for larger engine units (economies of scale). Manufacturers active in the 4–18 MW/unit range: Wärtsilä, MAN, GE Jenbacher, Caterpillar. — [Thunder Said Energy: Reciprocating gas engines: levelized costs?](https://thundersaidenergy.com/downloads/reciprocating-gas-engines-levelized-costs/)

### Inferences
- Even using the most favorable fuel cell figures found (DOE's $1,000/kW 2030 *target*, or the unverified $800/kW rule of thumb), fuel cells are priced in a broadly similar band to reciprocating gas engines (~$1,500/kW) and to gas turbines even after their 2024–2025 price surge (~$728–1,544/kW, heading toward ~$600/kW by 2027 per WoodMac — note this $600/kW figure is itself confusingly *lower* than the 2025 CT range cited by Gas Turbine World, so these two turbine sources may be using different cost boundaries or turbine classes; flagged as a conflict, not resolved in this session).
- Several fuel cell CAPEX figures found are R&D program *targets/aspirations* for future years, not confirmed as-delivered project costs — this is a material caveat the report writer should flag; a genuine "current, delivered project $/kW" figure for MW-scale PAFC/MCFC/SOFC/PEMFC installations (e.g., an actual FuelCell Energy or Doosan or Bloom Energy project cost) was not found despite multiple targeted searches (SEC filings, investor releases).

### Gaps
- No verified as-built $/kW figures for FuelCell Energy (MCFC), Doosan (PAFC), or Bloom Energy (SOFC) commercial projects were found in this session, despite searching SEC filings and investor press releases directly.
- No DOE, NREL, IEA, or IRENA report with an explicit, dated, cross-chemistry (PAFC/MCFC/SOFC/PEMFC) $/kW comparison table was located; the IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2024/2025 assumptions annexes were identified but contain electrolyzer costs, not fuel-cell power-generation costs, per the retrieved search snippets (not independently confirmed by direct fetch).
- The apparent conflict between Gas Turbine World's 2025 CT cost range ($728–1,544/kW) and Wood Mackenzie's ~$600/kW-by-2027 figure was not reconciled — these may reflect different turbine classes, scopes (equipment-only vs. EPC), or reporting conventions; flagged for the report writer rather than resolved here.
- No reciprocating-engine-specific source broken out by fuel type (NG vs. H2/ammonia-capable) with a $/kW figure was found; the $1,500/kW figure is a general NG reciprocating engine rule of thumb, not H2/ammonia-specific.

## What is the electrical efficiency ceiling/typical range for each fuel cell chemistry at utility scale, and how does it compare with gas turbines (simple/combined cycle) and reciprocating gas engines?

### Takeaway
Utility/multi-MW-scale fuel cells report electrical efficiencies in roughly the 47–65% range depending on chemistry and operating mode, generally matching or exceeding simple-cycle gas turbine efficiency (35–46% LHV, per this project's existing reference) and approaching combined-cycle gas turbine efficiency, though "once-through" vs. "fuel-recycled" configurations materially change the reported number for MCFC. LHV vs. HHV basis is inconsistently stated across sources and must be checked per figure.

### Cited Findings
- FuelCell Energy's SureSource 4000 (MCFC, three MW-class modules) is rated at **3.7 MW net AC power at 60% efficiency, LHV basis (of natural gas fuel)**. — [FuelCell Energy: Fuel Cell Efficiency Explained](https://www.fuelcellenergy.com/blog/fuel-cell-efficiency)
- A separate MCFC citation states "once-through" LHV thermal efficiency of ~47% (LHV basis), rising to 70–75% if unconverted fuel species are recycled back into the system — illustrating that MCFC efficiency figures are highly configuration-dependent and must specify once-through vs. recycled operation. — [ScienceDirect/related review, via search aggregation — primary article not independently confirmed](https://www.linquip.com/blog/efficiency-of-fuel-cell/) (secondary aggregator; original ScienceDirect source not directly fetched in this session)
- Bloom Energy's SOFC "Energy Server 6.5" is rated at **65% down to 53% efficiency, net AC, LHV basis**, depending on load/configuration, per the company's most recent datasheet reference found via search (not independently fetched — bloomenergy.com blocked in this session; content unverified beyond search snippet). Corresponding **HHV heat rate of 5,811–7,127 Btu/kWh (6,131–7,519 kJ/kWh)** was also cited for the same product. — [Bloom Energy Server 6.5 datasheet, Feb 2026 (search-indexed, not directly verified)](https://www.bloomenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/bloom-energy-server-feb-2026.pdf)
- Bloom Energy separately announced (per press release, Aug 2024) a hydrogen-fueled SOFC achieving **60% electrical efficiency on hydrogen fuel, plus 90% total efficiency in high-temperature combined heat and power (CHP) mode**; LHV/HHV basis for this specific hydrogen figure was not explicitly confirmed in the retrieved search snippet (bloomenergy.com itself blocked; press coverage retrieved instead). — [Green Car Congress: Bloom Energy announces hydrogen solid oxide fuel cell with 60% electrical efficiency](https://www.greencarcongress.com/2024/08/20240806-bloom.html)
- Bloom's SOFC also offers cumulative thermal efficiency >36% (exhaust heat available at >350°C) on top of electrical output, for total efficiency >90% in CHP mode — consistent with the hydrogen CHP figure above but reported for the general (not necessarily hydrogen-specific) product line. — [Green Car Congress, as above](https://www.greencarcongress.com/2024/08/20240806-bloom.html)

### Inferences
- Utility-scale fuel cell electrical efficiency (47–65% depending on chemistry/config, LHV basis in most cases found) generally meets or exceeds this project's existing simple-cycle gas turbine/engine reference range (35–46% LHV) and is in the same range as combined-cycle gas turbines (typically ~55–63% LHV for modern CCGT, though no CCGT figure was independently re-verified in this session — using this project's own reference framework).
- MCFC's efficiency figures span a very wide range (47% once-through to 70–75% with fuel recycling, or 60% as a single vendor-rated commercial product) — any comparison table must specify which of these operating modes a cited number refers to, since they are not directly comparable.

### Gaps
- No efficiency figures specifically for PAFC or PEMFC at multi-MW utility scale were found in this session (only unit-size data, see footprint section); PAFC (Doosan PureCell-class, ~400 kW units) and multi-MW PEMFC (Toyota/NREL 1 MW demo) efficiency numbers were not located despite searches.
- No figure found that is unambiguously HHV-basis and hydrogen-fuel-specific (as opposed to natural-gas-fuel or unspecified fuel) for any chemistry except the Bloom Energy 60% hydrogen SOFC figure, whose LHV/HHV basis is itself not confirmed from the retrieved source.
- This project's own reference range for combined-cycle gas turbine efficiency was not restated/verified here per the assignment's instruction to treat existing project comparators as given, so no independent CCGT efficiency citation is included — the report writer should pull that figure from the project's existing gas turbine research notes rather than from this file.

## Specifically for HYDROGEN-fueled (not natural-gas-fueled) fuel cells at MW+ scale: what real-world efficiency and cost data exists?

### Takeaway
Confirmed real-world, multi-MW, pure/byproduct-hydrogen-fueled fuel cell power plants exist and are operating (notably in South Korea), but detailed public efficiency and cost figures specific to the hydrogen-fuel mode of these plants are largely unavailable — most published efficiency numbers (e.g., Bloom Energy's 60% hydrogen SOFC figure) come from vendor product announcements rather than independently audited plant performance data, and no dataset comparing H2-fed vs. NG-fed efficiency/cost for the same fuel cell chemistry was found.

### Cited Findings
- The **Daesan Hydrogen Fuel Cell Power Plant** (Hanwha Energy / Doosan Fuel Cell, Seosan, South Korea) is described as "the largest industrial hydrogen fuel cell power plant in the world and the first to use only hydrogen recycled from petrochemical manufacturing" — i.e., a genuine byproduct-hydrogen-fed (not NG-reformed) fuel cell plant. Capacity: 50 MW (114 × 440 kW PAFC-type units per Doosan's PureCell platform), commissioned/operational as of 2020, generating up to 400,000 MWh/year (enough for ~160,000 Korean households). Hydrogen feedstock: byproduct H2 from the adjacent Hanwha Total Petrochemical plant (up to 3 tonnes H2/hour available). — [PowerMag: Innovative Byproduct-Hydrogen Fuel Cell Power Plant Completed](https://www.powermag.com/innovative-byproduct-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant-completed/); [Hanwha: Hanwha Energy Celebrates Its Completion of the World's First and Largest Byproduct-Hydrogen-Fuel-Cell Power Plant](https://www.hanwha.com/newsroom/news/press-releases/hanwha-energy-celebrates-its-completion-of-the-world-s-first-and-largest-byproduct-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant.do)
- No specific electrical efficiency (%) or CAPEX ($/kW) figure for the Daesan plant was found in the retrieved sources — coverage focuses on capacity, output (MWh/yr), households served, and water byproduct volume, not efficiency/cost.
- Bloom Energy's Aug 2024 announcement of a hydrogen-fueled SOFC at 60% electrical efficiency (see efficiency section above) is a **vendor product/technology announcement**, not confirmed independent third-party field data, and its scale (single unit/module vs. plant-scale MW deployment) was not clarified in the retrieved search snippet. — [Green Car Congress](https://www.greencarcongress.com/2024/08/20240806-bloom.html)
- Toyota/NREL are jointly building a 1 MW PEM fuel cell + 1.25 MW PEM electrolyzer + 600 kg H2 storage demonstration system at NREL's Flatirons Campus (Arvada, Colorado, USA) — explicitly a hydrogen-fed (electrolytic, not byproduct) PEMFC system at MW scale, but described as a research/demonstration platform (3-year, $6.5M DOE-HFTO-funded collaboration), not a commercial power plant; no efficiency or cost figures for its fuel cell mode were found. — [Toyota Newsroom: Toyota, NREL Collaborate to Advance Megawatt-Scale Fuel Cell Systems](https://pressroom.toyota.com/toyota-nrel-collaborate-to-advance-megawatt-scale-fuel-cell-systems/); [NREL: New Research Collaboration To Advance Megawatt-Scale Hydrogen Fuel Cell Systems](https://www.nrel.gov/news/detail/program/2022/new-research-collaboration-to-advance-megawatt-scale-hydrogen-fuel-cell-systems)
- Toyota's Long Beach, California tri-generation plant (announced for ~2020 startup) was planned at 2.35 MW electrical capacity plus 1.2 tonnes H2/day production, described as a "tri-gen" (using biogas-derived hydrogen via on-site reforming, not imported/pure H2 as the primary fuel per the retrieved snippet) — fuel pathway for this plant is NOT confirmed as pure hydrogen feed in the retrieved source and should be checked further before citing as a pure-H2 example. — [Toyota: Collaborating to Advance Fuel Cell Systems for Stationary Power Generation](https://www.toyota.com/usa/environmentalsustainability/carbon/collaborating-to-advance-fuel-cell-systems-for-stationary-power-generation)

### Inferences
- The clearest real-world example of a genuinely hydrogen-fed (not NG-reformed) multi-MW fuel cell power plant is the Daesan (Hanwha/Doosan, South Korea) plant, because it uses byproduct hydrogen directly rather than reforming natural gas on-site — but it is a PAFC-type technology, and no efficiency/cost figure specific to it was found, so it cannot be used to populate a quantitative comparison table.
- The data gap the assignment anticipated (narrower dataset for H2-fired vs. NG-fired fuel cells) is confirmed: essentially all quantitative efficiency/cost figures found in this research (Bloom Energy 60–65%, FuelCell Energy 60%/47%, DOE/EU CAPEX targets) are either NG-fuel-basis or unspecified-fuel-basis; the one clear hydrogen-specific efficiency figure (Bloom's 60% H2 SOFC) is a vendor announcement without independent verification or clear plant-scale context.

### Gaps
- No independently verified (non-vendor) efficiency percentage for any real hydrogen-fed fuel cell plant at MW+ scale was found.
- No CAPEX $/kW figure for the Daesan plant, the Toyota Long Beach plant, or any other named hydrogen-fed fuel cell project was found.
- Could not confirm whether Toyota's Long Beach plant is genuinely pure-hydrogen-fed or uses on-site reforming of biogas/renewable gas to hydrogen — this distinction matters for the project's "carrier medium" permutation logic and should be resolved with further research before citing this project as a hydrogen-fuel-cell precedent.

## What is the TRL/commercial maturity of hydrogen-fed stationary fuel cells at multi-MW scale, versus ammonia/hydrogen gas turbines and gas engines?

### Takeaway
Stationary fuel cells overall (mostly NG-fueled) are commercially mature (TRL 9) for backup power and CHP applications up to tens of MW, with South Korea running the world's largest stationary fuel cell fleet — but genuinely hydrogen-fed (not NG-reformed) multi-MW fuel cell deployment is a narrower, less mature slice of that fleet. No source found in this session explicitly ranks hydrogen fuel cells against ammonia/hydrogen gas turbines and gas engines on a single TRL scale; Singapore's own reference project (Keppel Sakra Cogen) has chosen a hydrogen-capable gas turbine, not a fuel cell, as its near-term commercial pathway, which is indirect evidence supporting this project's existing finding that gas turbines are ahead of fuel cells (and gas engines) for stationary duty at this scale.

### Cited Findings
- General TRL literature (aggregated via search, not independently fetched from a single primary TRL table) describes fuel cells for light/heavy-duty road transport as TRL 9 ("market uptake"/commercialized), while noting stationary applications such as backup power and micro-CHP have "gained or approached technical maturity" — language suggesting stationary is mature but with less precision than the transport-sector TRL 9 claim. — [ResearchGate/IEA Advanced Fuel Cells TRL Assessment, via search aggregation](https://ieafuelcell.com/output/trl/) (page not independently fetched; content from search snippet only)
- Low-temperature ammonia cracking technology (relevant if any MYSGH2 permutation considers on-site NH3-to-H2 cracking feeding a fuel cell rather than a turbine) is cited as **TRL 4** (early prototype stage) in an ammonia-fuel-cell review — this is markedly less mature than commercial ammonia/hydrogen combustion turbines. — [ScienceDirect: Ammonia-based processes integrated with fuel cells for electrical power generation: a review (title/abstract only; full text not fetched in this session)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S136403212500807X)
- Singapore's flagship near-term hydrogen power project — the Keppel Sakra Cogen (KSC) Plant on Jurong Island — is a **600 MW advanced combined-cycle GAS TURBINE (CCGT)**, built by Mitsubishi Power and Jurong Engineering, designed to run on natural gas initially and to be capable of 30% hydrogen blend with a pathway to 100% hydrogen; expected completion 1H 2026. This is a turbine solution, not a fuel cell solution, chosen for Singapore's first hydrogen-ready commercial power plant. — [Keppel: Keppel to develop Singapore's first hydrogen-ready power plant](https://www.keppel.com/media/keppel-to-develop-singapores-first-hydrogen-ready-power-plant/); [Mitsubishi Power: Keppel, together with Mitsubishi Power and Jurong Engineering consortium, breaks ground](https://power.mhi.com/regions/apac/news/gro20230719)
- A separate Singapore pilot combines a 55–65 MW **direct ammonia-fed combined cycle gas turbine** with a 100,000-tonne ammonia bunkering facility, as part of Singapore's National Hydrogen Strategy's push to trial technologies "on the cusp of commercial readiness" — again a turbine (not fuel cell) technology choice for ammonia-to-power. — [Ammonia Energy Association: Finalists revealed for ammonia bunkering, power generation pilot on Jurong Island](https://ammoniaenergy.org/articles/finalists-revealed-for-ammonia-bunkering-power-generation-pilot-on-jurong-island/); [EDB Singapore: New hydrogen-ready power plant to be built by 2026](https://www.edb.gov.sg/en/business-insights/insights/new-hydrogen-ready-power-plant-to-be-built-by-2026-as-singapore-seeks-greener-energy-generators.html)
- South Korea has, per one JRC-linked/search-aggregated figure, the world's largest stationary fuel cell fleet, driven by its Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS) and hydrogen policy, with multiple multi-MW to near-100 MW plants commissioned by Doosan Fuel Cell, Hanwha Energy, and Bloom Energy/SK E&C/SK Eternix partnerships (Daesan 50 MW; Shinincheon Bitdream 78.96 MW — described as "world's largest fuel cell plant" by capacity; Hwasung/Paju Bloom Energy sites totaling 28 MW; a further 80 MW Bloom/SK Eternix project targeted for 2025 commercial operation). — [World-Energy: New 78.96MW Hydrogen Fuel Cell Power Plant Opens in South Korea](https://www.world-energy.org/article/20949.html); [Doosan Fuel Cell media center](https://www.doosanfuelcell.com/en/media-center/medi-0101_view/?id=57); [Bloom Energy: Bloom Energy and SK E&C Announce 28-Megawatt Deployment](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-and-sk-ec-announce-28-megawatt-deployment-of-fuel-cell-technology-to-power-south-koreas-historic-hwasung-and-paju-cities/) (bloomenergy.com not independently fetched in this session, content from search snippet)
- NOTE ON FUEL SOURCE: most of the South Korean plants named above (Shinincheon Bitdream, Hwasung, Paju, the 2025 SK Eternix project) are described in search results as "hydrogen fuel cell power plant[s]" but the retrieved snippets do not confirm whether they run on natural-gas-reformed hydrogen (internal reformer, most common for commercial MCFC/PAFC/SOFC CHP plants) or externally supplied/byproduct hydrogen as Daesan does. Only Daesan is explicitly confirmed (via multiple sources) as byproduct-H2-fed. This distinction is important and should be treated as an open item, not assumed.

### Inferences
- The fact that Singapore's own flagship hydrogen-ready and ammonia-ready commercial-scale power projects (Keppel Sakra Cogen; the ammonia-fed CCGT pilot) both chose gas turbine technology rather than fuel cells is indirect but concrete evidence, from the exact market this project targets, that gas turbines are currently viewed as the more commercially ready pathway for utility-scale hydrogen/ammonia power in this region — consistent with this project's existing finding that turbines are ahead of gas engines, and by extension appear to be ahead of (or at least preferred over) fuel cells for this specific application and scale.
- South Korea's large stationary fuel cell fleet demonstrates that fuel cells ARE commercially deployed at significant aggregate scale (100s of MW nationally) for grid power, which is a materially higher maturity level than gas engines running on ammonia/H2 appear to have reached (per this project's existing research) — but this Korean fleet is predominantly NG-fueled or NG-reformed, and only one plant (Daesan, PAFC, 50 MW) is confirmed genuinely hydrogen(byproduct)-fed, so "fuel cells are commercially mature" and "hydrogen-fed fuel cells are commercially mature" are not the same claim and should not be conflated in the final report.

### Gaps
- No single source was found that places hydrogen-fed fuel cells, ammonia/H2 gas turbines, and ammonia/H2 gas engines on one explicit TRL scale for direct three-way comparison; the ordering suggested above (turbines ≥ fuel cells > gas engines, for this specific ammonia/H2 stationary-duty context) is this researcher's inference from indirect evidence (Singapore's own technology choices, Korea's fleet composition), not a directly cited ranking, and should be flagged as an inference in the final report, not stated as a sourced fact.
- Could not confirm the exact fuel source (external H2 supply/byproduct vs. internal NG reforming) for most named Korean "hydrogen fuel cell" plants other than Daesan — this materially affects how directly comparable they are to this project's MMHE-cracked, pipeline-delivered pure-hydrogen supply scenario.
- No Japan- or Malaysia-specific utility-scale (>1 MW) hydrogen-fed fuel cell power plant was found in this session (Toyota's Japan installation, Honsha Plant, is only 100 kW — well below utility/MW+ scale).

### Inferences
- Utility-scale hydrogen-fed fuel cell deployment, where the fuel is verifiably hydrogen (not natural gas reformed internally) rather than a "hydrogen fuel cell" plant that is merely branded around hydrogen as an end-state pathway, is essentially limited to one confirmed example worldwide at meaningful commercial scale (Daesan, South Korea, 50 MW, PAFC) plus one US demonstration-scale project (Toyota/NREL, 1 MW). This is a very thin evidence base for the MYSGH2 project's own >1 MW pure-hydrogen-fed context, and strengthens the case (alongside Singapore's own gas-turbine technology choices) that fuel cells are not yet the leading commercial option for utility-scale imported/cracked hydrogen power in this region.
- No Malaysian precedent exists for any stationary fuel cell power plant, so MYSGH2 would be a first-of-kind for Malaysia regardless of which power generation technology (fuel cell or turbine) is ultimately selected for the Singapore offtake side.
