# Fuel cells trail turbines for this route

Four fuel cell chemistries compete for stationary power today — **PAFC, MCFC, SOFC and PEMFC** — led respectively by HyAxiom (formerly Doosan Fuel Cell), FuelCell Energy, Bloom Energy, and a fragmented PEMFC field (Plug Power, Ballard, Cummins/Accelera, Hyundai HTWO, Toshiba H2Rex). Only two commercial products anywhere are sold as **dedicated pure-hydrogen units** — HyAxiom's PureCell M400 Hydrogen (PAFC, 440 kW, ~50% electrical efficiency) and Bloom Energy's Hydrogen-Powered Energy Server (SOFC, 300 kW+, ~52% average/60% peak) — and neither has a multi-MW, long-duration, independently verified hydrogen-fuel operating record; most of the sector's deployed capacity, including South Korea's 800 MW–1 GW+ stationary fuel cell fleet (the world's largest), runs on natural gas reformed on-site, not delivered hydrogen ([FuelCell Energy platform page](https://www.fuelcellenergy.com/platform/fuel-cell-power-plants); [HyAxiom M400 Hydrogen datasheet](https://www.doosan.com/images/media-center/ces2024/(Data%20Sheet)%20HyAxiom%20PureCell%20Model%20400%20Hydrogen.pdf); [Bloom Hydrogen Data Sheet](https://www.bloomenergy.com/resource/hydrogen-spec-sheet/)). The one confirmed multi-MW plant genuinely fed by hydrogen rather than reformed gas is Korea's 50 MW Daesan plant, and it runs on industrial by-product hydrogen, not imported or ammonia-cracked hydrogen ([PowerMag](https://www.powermag.com/innovative-byproduct-hydrogen-fuel-cell-power-plant-completed/)). Singapore itself has already chosen technology for its two flagship hydrogen/ammonia power projects — the 600 MW Keppel Sakra Cogen plant and a 55–65 MW ammonia-fed pilot — and both are **gas turbines, not fuel cells** ([Keppel](https://www.keppel.com/media/keppel-to-develop-singapores-first-hydrogen-ready-power-plant/); [Ammonia Energy Association](https://ammoniaenergy.org/articles/finalists-revealed-for-ammonia-bunkering-power-generation-pilot-on-jurong-island/)). For the MYSGH2 concept — importing green ammonia to MMHE, cracking it to hydrogen, and piping that hydrogen to Singapore for power — this means fuel cells are technically plausible as an offtake-side generation technology (their electrical efficiencies of 41–65% LHV generally match or beat simple-cycle gas turbines) but sit behind gas turbines in commercial readiness for grid-scale, pure-hydrogen duty in this exact region, and no Malaysia or Singapore deployment of a hydrogen-fed fuel cell exists to de-risk the choice.

## No fuel cell chemistry is purpose-built for imported, cracked hydrogen at MW scale

The four chemistries split cleanly by temperature, fuel tolerance and typical unit size. **PAFC** (150–220°C, platinum catalyst, high CO tolerance) is dominated by HyAxiom's PureCell M400 family — 440–460 kW building-block units, sold in NG, LPG/NG dual-fuel, and dedicated Hydrogen variants, with HyAxiom having shipped a cumulative ~330.9 MW across ~758 units historically ([BCC Research company profile](https://www.bccresearch.com/company-index/profile/doosan-fuel-cell-co-ltd)). Fuji Electric remains a much smaller, Japan-centric PAFC player at the 100 kW class (FP-100E/FP-100i), with no evidence of MW-class installations. **MCFC** (550–650°C, internal reforming, no precious metal) is essentially a one-company field: FuelCell Energy's SureSource platform, sold in modules from ~300 kW to 3.7 MW and arrayed into "parks" up to 58.8 MW (the Gyeonggi Green Energy plant in Hwaseong-si, South Korea, still under a FuelCell Energy long-term service agreement as of FY2025) ([FuelCell Energy FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/886128/000110465925122302/fcel-20251031x10k.htm)). Crucially, FuelCell Energy's currently-shipping carbonate line runs on natural gas, biogas, or NG/H2 blends up to 40–50% H2 — **not pure hydrogen**; a 100%-hydrogen product is described as "in development" via a separate solid oxide line, so the MCFC platform as sold today cannot directly consume MMHE's cracked hydrogen stream without blending it back down with natural gas ([FuelCell Energy blog](https://www.fuelcellenergy.com/blog/since-ceraweek-advancing-low-carbon-power-and-hydrogen)).

**SOFC** (600–1000°C) is led by Bloom Energy, the only vendor with a clearly commercialized, spec-sheet-level pure-hydrogen product: the Hydrogen-Powered Energy Server, rated 300 kW and above on ≥99.90%-purity hydrogen, at an average 52% (initial maximum 60%) electrical efficiency ([Bloom Hydrogen Data Sheet](https://www.bloomenergy.com/resource/hydrogen-spec-sheet/)). Bloom's base commercial module is 325 kW, aggregated into MW-scale plants, with a fleet exceeding 400 MW cumulative in South Korea via SK ecoplant and additional deployments in Taiwan (10 MW, Unimicron) ([Bloom/SK ecoplant 500 MW agreement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1664703/000162828023042553/ex991.htm); [Bloom Taiwan installation](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-installs-first-fuel-cells-in-taiwan/)). Mitsubishi Power offers a fundamentally different product — a pressurized SOFC integrated with a micro gas turbine — demonstrated at 206 kW AC output and 50.2% measured thermal efficiency over a 4,000-hour continuous test, but documented only on hydrocarbon fuel, with a single Tokyo commercial installation (Marunouchi Building, 2018) ([MHI](https://www.mhi.com/news/1309201714.html)). Ceres Power, Convion, SolydEra, Elcogen and Sunfire round out the SOFC field as licensors or sub-100 kW players; several (Convion C60, SolydEra) offer hydrogen as a configurable fuel option, but only at pilot/CHP scale.

**PEMFC** (60–80°C) is the most fragmented chemistry commercially, with no single dominant utility-scale OEM. Plug Power's GenSure HP (built from 125 kW ProGen engines, configured at 500 kW–1.5 MW) and Ballard's FCgen-XD/heavy-duty modules (70–405 kW net) target data-center and grid-support backup power but remain at pilot stage for MW-class deployment; Cummins/Accelera operates a 1 MW PEM plant on surplus hydrogen at a South Korean refinery; Hyundai's HTWO 100 kW generator is the most fully specified PEMFC unit found, running on ISO 14687-2/SAE J2719-spec hydrogen at 7–9 bar supply with ≥50% LHV efficiency ([HTWO Fuel Cell Solution PDF](https://ecv.hyundai.com/resources/assets/download/HTWO_Fuel_Cell_Solution_ENG.pdf)). Panasonic's ENE-FARM and Toshiba's H2Rex (a genuinely pure-hydrogen PEFC, stacked in 100 kW modules up to a 500 kW array at Tanaka Kikinzoku's Shonan Plant) sit at sub-MW, distributed-generation scale. Because ISO 14687 caps ammonia at just 0.1 ppm for PEMFC-grade hydrogen, any residual NH3 slip from imperfect cracking at MMHE is a first-order fuel-quality risk specifically for this chemistry, more so than for the higher-temperature PAFC/MCFC/SOFC options.

Two patterns cut across this table that matter for MYSGH2. First, **no OEM in any chemistry publishes a current (2024–2026), primary-source $/kW installed cost** — every figure found is either a stale historical data point (Bloom's 2018 S-1 filing), an aspirational DOE/EU program target, or absent entirely; this is a genuine data gap, not an oversight, and any CAPEX assumption used in downstream LCOH modeling must be logged in memory.md as an assumption per CLAUDE.md §7. Second, **footprint data is almost as thin**: only FuelCell Energy (MCFC) and, weakly, Bloom Energy (SOFC) have any plant-level footprint figure, and even those are vendor-sourced or third-party-derived rather than independently verified by a body like NREL, DOE or IEA.

## Efficiency and footprint against this project's gas turbine and gas engine baseline

Set against this project's existing gas turbine reference range (simple-cycle 35–46% LHV, CCGT ~55–63% LHV, footprint ~0.111–0.139 acres/MW or ~450–563 m²/MW) and reciprocating gas engines (~$1,500/kW capital rule-of-thumb), fuel cells hold their own on paper but not decisively. Electrical efficiency across the sourced fuel cell figures spans roughly **41–65% LHV**, comfortably above simple-cycle turbines and competitive with CCGT — Bloom's SOFC (54% lifetime average, up to 65% beginning-of-life) and FuelCell Energy's MCFC (~60%, basis unstated) both sit in or above the CCGT band, while PAFC (41–50%) and PEMFC (≥50%) trail somewhat but still beat simple-cycle turbines. The one directly comparable footprint figure — FuelCell Energy's 59 MW MCFC park at ~5.2 acres, or ≈0.088 acres/MW — is modestly more compact than this project's gas turbine CCGT range, consistent with the industry's "modular, no large rotating equipment" claim, but it is a single vendor-linked data point, not an independent siting study, and no comparable figure exists for PAFC, SOFC or PEMFC at multi-MW scale. On cost, fuel cells do **not** look advantaged: even the most favorable fuel cell figures (DOE's $1,000/kW 2030 target, or an unverified $800/kW industry rule of thumb) land in the same band as 2025 gas turbine capital costs ($728–1,544/kW per Gas Turbine World) and reciprocating engines (~$1,500/kW), while most sourced fuel cell CAPEX figures (€5,000–18,400/kW for various SOFC/PEMFC configurations) are considerably higher. Combined with the fact that no independent TRL ranking places hydrogen-fed fuel cells against ammonia/hydrogen turbines or engines on a single scale, the strongest available evidence for relative maturity is behavioral: **Singapore itself, when it had a real commercial decision to make, chose gas turbines for both its flagship hydrogen-ready plant (600 MW Keppel Sakra Cogen) and its ammonia-fed pilot (55–65 MW)**, not fuel cells — a signal from the exact offtake market this project targets.

## Singapore has fuel cell activity, but none of it burns imported hydrogen

Asia-Pacific is where the fuel cell industry's real commercial weight sits, and Singapore specifically has more fuel cell activity than the MYSGH2 concept might assume — just not the kind this project needs. **South Korea** hosts the world's largest stationary fuel cell fleet (reported at roughly 800 MW to over 1 GW as of 2025, figures not reconciled between sources), built almost entirely on natural-gas-fed PAFC/MCFC under a Renewable Portfolio Standard that gives fuel cells a favorable REC weighting — though that policy is itself now under revision, with a bill to scrap the RPS system advancing as of May 2026 ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/05/20/south-korea-advances-bill-to-scrap-renewable-quota-system/)). Within that fleet, only the **50 MW Daesan plant** (Hanwha/Doosan, PAFC, by-product hydrogen from a petrochemical complex) is confirmed as genuinely hydrogen-fed rather than NG-reformed; other large Korean "hydrogen fuel cell" plants (Shinincheon Bitdream at 78.96 MW, Bloom/SK ecoplant sites) carry hydrogen branding but their actual fuel source was not confirmed in the research. **Singapore** has one confirmed fuel cell deployment — SK ecoplant is bringing Bloom Energy SOFC units to a GDS data center, described as Bloom's first Singapore project — but it is natural-gas-fed, not hydrogen-fed, and its fuel-cell-specific MW rating was not disclosed ([Bloom Energy](https://www.bloomenergy.com/blog/singapore-data-center-to-expand-capacity-with-bloom-energy-fuel-cells/)). A second, more directly relevant data point is **DayOne's 20 MW Singapore data center**, which broke ground with an explicit 0.3 MW SOFC hydrogen proof-of-concept and a Sembcorp partnership tied to Singapore's Green Data Centre Roadmap ([Hydrogen Central](https://hydrogen-central.com/dayone-breaks-ground-on-green-ai-data-centre-in-singapore-20mw-site-will-trial-solid-oxide-fuel-cell-sofc-hydrogen-power/)) — the closest thing found anywhere in the region to a hydrogen-fed SOFC pilot, but it is explicitly a proof-of-concept at sub-MW scale, not a commercial power plant. **Taiwan** has a confirmed but NG-fed Bloom Energy SOFC deployment (up to 10 MW at Unimicron). **Malaysia has no fuel cell power generation deployment of any kind, at any scale, on any fuel, in any of the research notes reviewed** — its hydrogen activity is confined to production/export ambitions (Sarawak's Kuching and Bintulu projects) and small mobility-refuelling pilots, not stationary power. This is worth stating plainly for this project's own siting: **no Malaysia deployment and no Singapore hydrogen-fed fuel cell deployment were found anywhere in this research** — the closest regional precedents (Daesan's by-product hydrogen, DayOne's 0.3 MW proof-of-concept) are both smaller in scale, different in feedstock origin (industrial by-product vs. ammonia-cracked/imported), or both, compared to what MYSGH2 would require.

## Conclusion

The research converges on a clear, if unwelcome, finding for anyone hoping fuel cells offer a ready-made offtake technology for MMHE's cracked hydrogen: the commercial fuel cell industry has built its scale on natural gas, not hydrogen, and where it has touched real hydrogen it has been industrial by-product streams (Daesan) or small pilots (DayOne), never imported, ammonia-derived hydrogen delivered by pipeline at the multi-MW-plus scale this project envisions. That gap is not fatal — Bloom's SOFC and HyAxiom's PAFC both sell catalog pure-hydrogen products today, and fuel cell electrical efficiencies compare favorably with gas turbines on paper — but it means any MYSGH2 permutation that specifies fuel cells for Singapore-side power generation would be pioneering a fuel pathway, not adopting a proven one, in a region where the dominant market participant (Singapore's own EMA-backed projects) has already picked gas turbines for both its hydrogen-ready and ammonia-ready flagship plants. The most actionable next step is not more literature search but direct vendor engagement: CAPEX, footprint, and hydrogen-specific efficiency data are thin enough across every chemistry that a request-for-information from HyAxiom, FuelCell Energy, and Bloom Energy — the three vendors with actual pure- or near-pure-hydrogen products — would likely surface more decision-useful numbers than any further desk research, and any figures adopted in the interim should be logged in memory.md as explicit assumptions per this project's No-Fabrication Rule.
